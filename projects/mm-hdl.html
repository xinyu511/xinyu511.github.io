Multimodal representations of hardware designs

A Verilog module can be read as code, as a dataflow graph, or through a plain-English description. We asked whether a model does better when it sees more than one of them.

Team of four; my part is described belowPython · PyTorch · Qwen3
14,167Verilog designs
8circuit categories
3views per design
1,424held-out test designs

The setup

Each design in our dataset (curated from the public BRIDGES corpus) comes with its Verilog code, the design's dataflow graph, a natural-language description, and one of eight category labels. We studied two tasks: finding the right design for a description, and classifying a design into its category.

My part

  • The LLM classification benchmark. Qwen3-8B, zero-shot, given the code, the graph, or both, with prompts that restrict the answer to one category name, constrained decoding, and tracking of inputs truncated by the context window.
  • The error analysis. Confusion matrices and an overlap study of which designs each input view gets right. This is where the case for combining views came from.
  • Pieces of the trained model. A graph-only pretraining stage with warm start for the graph encoder, a padding-aware pooling fix, and the cross-attention fusion experiments.

What the baselines showed

Neither view dominates. The graph alone is weaker overall, yet it is the only view that gets 130 designs right, so the two carry different information.

Where the team landed

The trained model encodes the graph with a graph attention network, fuses it with the code, and aligns the result with description embeddings using a contrastive loss. On description-to-design search over the full pool, the team's gated-fusion model reached recall@1 of 0.696, against 0.396 for BM25 over code and graph text. Those retrieval results are my teammates' work; the cross-attention variant I ran was still in progress at the poster session.
