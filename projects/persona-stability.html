Why role-play LLMs drift in long conversations

Give a model a persona and let it talk for 40 turns, and it stops sounding like that person. We expected forgetting. What we found was imitation.

Two-person team; I built most of the harness Python · MLX · Hugging Face · Qwen3
~225dialogues, 40–50 turns
~500blind LLM-judge scores
3,135published dialogues re-analyzed
24 / 24persona facts correct at turn 39

TL;DR. Over long conversations, a persona-conditioned model starts copying text that is already in its context, both its own earlier replies and its partner's, while it can still answer questions about the persona correctly. Training-free fixes that push the persona's logits back in did not help. Damping attention to the speaker's own old replies is the one intervention with a consistent positive sign.

The question

LLM-simulated users and role-play agents are only useful over a long horizon if they stay the person they were set up to be. Our original idea was a test-time, training-free fix: compare the next-token distribution with and without the persona in the prompt, treat the difference as the persona's contribution, and add a scaled part of it back once the persona fades.

What I built

  • Two inference backends (MLX on Apple silicon, Hugging Face on CUDA) with prefix-reusing KV-cache slots, so long dialogues don't re-encode history every turn. Both expose attention bias, hidden-state capture, and residual-stream steering, and load 4-bit and GGUF models.
  • Decoding-time interventions as plug-in strategies: persona re-injection, classifier-free-guidance-style logit contrast, a static persona logit bias with a drift thermostat, activation steering, and attention damping on the speaker's own history.
  • A two-speaker "dyad" setup where one model plays both personas, with separate histories, caches, and strategies per side.
  • A blind evaluation pipeline: dialogues split into anonymized 20-message chunks, scored by independent LLM judges. I calibrated it with planted errors (3/3 caught at the right message) and test-retest across judging phases.

What we found

The model locks into a template within about ten turns. On Qwen3-8B, the share of a reply's 3-word sequences already in its last five replies rose from 0.28 (turns 0–9) to 0.92 (turns 40–49), while next-token entropy fell from 0.41 to 0.03 nats.

It is imitation, not forgetting. When we asked the model a factual question about its persona at turn 39, it answered correctly 24 of 24 times (Qwen3-4B, 12 pairs). The persona is still in there; the model just prefers to copy what is already in context. This shows up with every partner we tried: replayed users, a published benchmark's live LLM user, and the same model on both sides.

Truncating history trades copying for continuity. Showing the model only the last 4 rounds cut partner-copying to 0.15 (vs. 0.33 baseline, 0.20 with attention damping). But a blind continuity judge, run three times, found more contradictions of earlier facts under truncation in 11 of 12 pairs: 3.6 vs. 1.8 per dialogue.

What didn't work

Our original idea, adding the persona's logit contribution back during decoding, did not improve judged behavior in any of four forms on three models. The distribution-level persona signal and the behavior a judge sees turned out to be decoupled, which is a useful negative result for anyone building drift monitors on logit statistics.

Next

The target has shifted from "persona forgetting" to conversational collapse between simulated speakers. Next steps are a drift sensor based on self-similarity of recent replies (Spearman ≈ −0.8 with judged fidelity in our runs) and a versioned API judge for final numbers.
